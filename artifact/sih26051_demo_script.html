Two-Person Demo Script

A spoken, live-demo script for presenting the working AreaTherm prototype — one voice carrying the technical approach (the physics engine, live data), the other carrying the analytical layer (optimization, sensitivity, validation). Built to be read aloud while actually operating the app, not just talked over slides.

00 How to use this script

This is a run-of-show, not a transcript to memorize word-for-word. Say it in your own voice — the beats, the order, and what's shown on screen at each moment are the part that matters.

  • Core runtime: ~8 minutes. Built for a working-prototype demo slot with time for a live walkthrough, not just a pitch. If you're given less time, cut Beat 6 (Sensitivity) and shorten Beat 9; if given more, let the live screens breathe — pause after each click and let the judges actually read the numbers.
  • Before you start: open the app to the Dashboard, confirm you have working internet (live weather needs it — see contingency below), and decide together which reference location you'll demo with (Leh is the natural choice — it's the brief's founding case).
  • One laptop, one operator: whichever presenter is speaking in a given beat should also be the one clicking — don't hand the keyboard back and forth mid-sentence.

01 Roles

Split by what each person is actually best positioned to defend under questioning — not an arbitrary 50/50.

Presenter A

Technical Lead

Owns the problem framing, the physics model, live data sourcing, and system architecture. Answers any question that starts with "how does the model actually..."

Presenter B

Analytical Lead

Owns the optimization method, sensitivity analysis, validation approach, and result interpretation. Answers any question that starts with "how do you know the recommendation is..."

2 · The Problem, Framed Technically

Presenter A

The DRDO brief is specific: shelters for extreme, high-altitude, remote regions. In practice, these get built to one generalized design, and that design is never checked against the actual site's climate.

Presenter A

The failure mode is always the same: high envelope heat loss, poor insulation choices, a window-to-wall ratio that wasn't sized for that latitude, and no thermal mass to carry daytime solar gain into the night. Every one of those is a design decision — which means every one of them is something a model can test before construction, not after.

Show: stay on Dashboard. Optional: gesture at the empty "Run a simulation to populate" state — it's an honest empty state, not a fabricated demo number.
1:15–2:15

3 · Live Data, Not a Hardcoded Demo

Presenter A

We're not going to show you a canned dataset. Watch — I'm selecting Leh, Ladakh right now, and this fetch is going out to two live sources: Open-Meteo for a real 7-day weather forecast, and NASA POWER for a twenty-year solar and temperature climatology.

Do: Navigate to Location & Climate (or Guided Setup Step 1). Select Leh, Ladakh. Click "Load Real Weather (Open-Meteo)." Wait for the fetch to complete (a few seconds) before continuing.
Presenter A

That's it loading now — hourly temperature and solar irradiance for the next week, averaged into a typical-day curve, plus a real twenty-year average for annual solar potential. No location in this app ships with pre-written climate numbers. Every one of the ten reference sites, from Ladakh to Nagpur, pulls this same way.

Show: the green "✓ Data source" badge and the ambient-temperature / solar-irradiance charts that just rendered. Point at the badge specifically — it's the app's running commitment that nothing here is illustrative.
2:15–3:30

4 · The Thermal Model, Proven On Screen

Presenter A

Underneath, this is a two-node resistance-capacitance heat-balance model — one node for indoor air, one for thermal mass — solved hour by hour. It's the same sol-air-temperature approach used in standard building energy analysis, not something we invented for this.

Do: Navigate to Thermal Simulation. Click "Run Thermal Simulation."
Presenter A

That's a full 24-hour simulation, just now, on this exact shelter design and this exact climate: solar gain, wall loss, roof loss, floor loss, ventilation loss, and what the thermal mass is charging and discharging — all resolved hour by hour.

Do: Scroll to the Heat Balance Detail table. Click "Explain calculation →" next to Heat loss — walls.
Presenter A

And this is the part I actually want to sit on: every number on this screen opens into its own formula, with the real numbers from this run substituted in. This isn't a black box producing a score you have to trust — it's a model you can audit, term by term.

Presenter B

Which is exactly the point where this stops being a simulation and starts being decision support — and that's mine to walk through.

3:30–4:30

5 · Optimization

Presenter B

One simulation tells you how one design performs. The actual design question is: out of every reasonable combination of orientation, insulation, glazing, and thermal mass — which one performs best, for this specific site?

Do: Navigate to Optimization. Click "Run Design Optimization."
Presenter B

That just generated and scored around sixty candidate designs — varying orientation, insulation thickness, window area, glazing type, and thermal mass — on a weighted formula: forty percent thermal comfort, twenty-five percent heat retention, fifteen percent solar utilization, and the rest split between energy demand and estimated cost. Those weights aren't fixed — a reviewer can drag them and re-rank instantly.

Show: the candidate table (Design A through E) and the green Recommended Shelter Design panel. Read one or two concrete numbers off it — orientation, insulation thickness, comfort score.
Presenter B

This is the recommendation — with the full comparison table right below it, so nobody has to take our word for why it won against the other four.

4:30–5:15

6 · Sensitivity Analysis

Presenter B

The next question a good reviewer asks is: of all those variables, which one actually matters most? That's what this chart answers.

Do: Scroll to Sensitivity Analysis. Point at the top bar.
Presenter B

Each bar is the same baseline design with one parameter improved, showing exactly how many points that single change moves the comfort score. Whichever bar is longest — that's not a guess, that's the design lever this specific site cares about most. It's genuine sensitivity analysis, not a fixed talking point we wrote in advance.

5:15–6:00

7 · Validation — Where We're Honest About the Gap

Presenter B

And here's the question we'd ask if we were sitting where you are: how do you know any of this matches reality? Right now — we don't, fully. No shelter built to this model has been instrumented yet. What we do have is the mechanism to check it the moment that data exists.

Do: Navigate to Validation. Point at the measured-data table and the error-metric cards (MAE / RMSE / MAPE / R²).
Presenter B

A field team enters measured indoor temperatures against this model's prediction, and it computes standard error metrics automatically — mean absolute error, RMSE, R-squared — plus a measured-versus-predicted plot. The rows on screen right now are placeholders, clearly labelled as such. The instrument is real; the field data is the next step, not a claim we're making today.

Presenter B

03 If the demo breaks

No internet / live fetch fails on stage

  • Open-Meteo results are cached in the browser for 7 days — if you ran the same location once beforehand on the same machine, a second click at Beat 3 will resolve instantly from cache rather than failing.
  • If the fetch genuinely fails live, don't stall on it: say plainly, "that's a live network call and it just failed — here's what it looks like when it succeeds," and switch to a second browser tab already holding a completed run from rehearsal. Name it as a rehearsal run, not this session's data.
  • Never claim a cached or pre-run number as if it were fetched live in front of the judges — the whole pitch rests on "nothing here is illustrative"; one fudged number undermines that instantly.

04 Q&A prep

Answer assigned by who actually owns that part of the system — hand off cleanly rather than both talking at once.

AWhy not use EnergyPlus, TRNSYS, or ANSYS instead of building your own model?

Those are valid full-building tools, and heavier than this problem needs for a first design-screening pass. Our model uses the same underlying building-physics principle — sol-air temperature, resistance-capacitance heat balance — but stays transparent and fast enough to evaluate sixty candidate designs in seconds, with every term auditable on screen. That trade favors rapid design comparison over single-run precision, and it's an explicit, disclosed choice, not a shortcut we're hiding.

BHow do you know the recommended design is actually correct?

We don't claim field-validated accuracy — we claim a transparent, documented method and a working validation mechanism ready for the moment field data exists. That's exactly what the Validation screen you saw is for.

AWhat happens to this if the internet is down at the actual construction site?

The live-fetch step happens once, during design — not during operation. A designer runs this on a connected machine before construction to pick the design; the shelter itself needs no connectivity afterward.

BAre the optimization weights meaningful, or just for show?

They're live — dragging the weight sliders and re-running changes which candidate is recommended, in front of you, because the score is a real weighted sum over the same computed metrics, not a fixed label attached after the fact.

AWhat's the plan to take this from prototype to production?

The current build is a dependency-free browser prototype so every claim we make is something you can watch run, right now, without a server. It's architected so the thermal engine — which has no framework dependencies today — ports directly onto a production backend (Spring Boot, MySQL) without a redesign, and that path is documented, not hand-waved.
